# *Animal Farm*: Plot, Allegory, and Themes

**Spoilers throughout.** George Orwell's *Animal Farm* (1945) is a political fable about a rebellion that overthrows an abusive ruler but fails to secure equality. The pigs gradually capture the new order through propaganda and force. The publisher describes the work as an allegory of revolution becoming totalitarian rule. Orwell's proposed preface, published later as “The Freedom of the Press,” explicitly identifies Stalinist Russia as its target.

## Plot summary

Mr. Jones neglects the animals on Manor Farm. Old Major, an elderly boar, inspires them to imagine life without human rule. After Major dies, pigs Napoleon and Snowball organize a revolt when Jones fails to feed the animals. They drive him away, rename the property Animal Farm, and adopt commandments promising solidarity and equality.

The pigs claim leadership and reserve milk and apples for themselves. Snowball promotes literacy and a windmill; Napoleon secretly raises puppies as attack dogs, then uses them to expel Snowball. Napoleon takes sole control, blames Snowball for setbacks, and later claims the windmill idea as his own. The animals work harder as pigs negotiate with humans and take further privileges. When the windmill is destroyed, Napoleon uses the crisis to demand more labor and obedience.

Dogs intimidate dissenters; animals make coerced confessions and are executed. The commandments are altered to excuse the pigs' conduct. Boxer, a hardworking horse, is injured and—despite promises of care—is sold to a slaughterer. At the end, pigs walk upright and dine with human farmers. The other animals look from the window and can no longer distinguish pigs from people.

## Characters and common historical readings

These are interpretive parallels, not a precise one-to-one code. Orwell compresses history and also critiques authoritarian power more broadly.

| Story element | Common association |
|---|---|
| Jones and the old farm | Tsarist ruling order and pre-revolutionary Russia. |
| Old Major | A composite revolutionary vision, often linked to Marx and Lenin. |
| Napoleon | Joseph Stalin and the consolidation of personal dictatorship. |
| Snowball | Leon Trotsky, Stalin's rival, expelled and exiled from the USSR. |
| Squealer and the dogs | Propaganda and coercive security institutions. |
| Boxer | Workers whose labor sustains the regime but who lack power to hold leaders accountable. |

The historical frame is the Russian Revolution and the later Soviet regime. The 1917 revolutions ended imperial rule and brought the Bolsheviks to power. After Lenin died in 1924, Stalin won the succession struggle; Trotsky was exiled, and the Great Terror of the 1930s used purges, forced confessions, executions, and labor camps against perceived enemies. These events illuminate Napoleon's purges and Snowball's expulsion, but the novel rearranges history for satire rather than documenting it chronologically.

## Major themes

- **Revolution can reproduce domination.** Removing an unjust ruler does not ensure justice; a new elite can replace the old one when power is unaccountable.
- **Propaganda rewrites reality.** Squealer's explanations, altered commandments, and animals' imperfect memories make exploitation appear legitimate.
- **Language and slogans can conceal injustice.** Repeated formulas replace debate when people cannot test claims or question their meaning.
- **Knowledge is political power.** The pigs' literacy gives them an advantage; most animals cannot check the written rules or official accounts.
- **Fear replaces consent.** Dogs and staged confessions suppress dissent, making apparent agreement a product of intimidation.

The book condemns Stalinist betrayal of revolutionary ideals; it should not be simplified into a claim that all socialist or egalitarian ideals are identical to Stalinist dictatorship. Orwell's proposed preface specifically criticizes uncritical defense of the Soviet regime.

## Reading the allegory carefully

Ask who controls food, education, rules, and the record of the past; which animals benefit from each policy; and why others accept or resist it. The windmill can suggest modernization, promised abundance, and labor mobilized through hopes repeatedly deferred—it need not have one fixed historical equivalent.

*Animal Farm* is a fable, not a comprehensive history of Russia or the Soviet Union. Read its historical correspondences alongside background sources, distinguishing the 1917 revolutions from Stalin's later rule.
